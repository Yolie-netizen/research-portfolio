# Three Futures: Ideal, Feasible, and Family-Approved

**Exploratory Qualitative Field Research | Ongoing**

## Overview

This project examines how career aspirations are formed and negotiated before students formally enter the labor market.

Focusing on a female first-year university student in Kashgar, Xinjiang, the study explores how personal aspirations interact with family expectations, gender norms, academic choices, geographic mobility, and perceptions of stability and safety.

Rather than assuming that family or local culture necessarily restricts women's choices, the project examines how family support, practical resources, social expectations, and individual agency jointly shape imagined career futures.

## Core Research Question

How does a female university student retrospectively describe the development of her career plans from secondary school through university entry, and what roles do family involvement, gender expectations, academic specialization, and geographic conditions play in this process?

## Conceptual Framework: Three Futures

The project distinguishes between three imagined occupational futures:

### Ideal Future
The career the participant would most like to pursue if constraints such as family expectations, location, income, and academic conditions were temporarily removed.

### Feasible Future
The career the participant currently considers realistically attainable given her academic background, available resources, labor-market conditions, and other practical constraints.

### Family-Approved Future
The career the participant believes would receive the strongest approval or support from her family.

Comparing these three futures provides a way to study how occupational aspirations are negotiated rather than treating stated career preferences as unconstrained individual choices.

## Research Design

The project uses a rapid qualitative case-study design developed for a short fieldwork period.

### Semi-Structured Interviews

Repeated interviews reconstruct the participant's career-planning trajectory from middle school through university entry.

Topics include:

- changing career aspirations;
- university and major selection;
- family participation in educational decisions;
- definitions of a "good" or "stable" job;
- gendered occupational expectations; and
- preferences regarding future work location.

### Career Timeline

The participant retrospectively identifies preferred and expected occupations at several stages of her educational trajectory and discusses why these preferences changed.

### Occupational Card Sorting

A set of occupations is ranked according to:

1. personal preference;
2. perceived attainability; and
3. anticipated family approval.

Differences between the rankings are used to identify potential tensions between aspiration, feasibility, and family expectations.

### Hypothetical Job Scenarios

The participant compares hypothetical jobs that vary in:

- location;
- salary;
- employment stability;
- housing;
- career advancement; and
- proximity to family.

Individual conditions are then changed to explore which trade-offs most strongly influence occupational choices.

### Participant Validation

A final follow-up interview is used to present preliminary interpretations to the participant and identify misunderstandings, contradictions, or missing context.

## Analytical Focus

The planned thematic analysis focuses on:

- changes in career aspirations;
- family participation and support;
- gender expectations;
- geographic mobility;
- perceptions of stability and safety;
- practical constraints;
- negotiation and compromise; and
- individual agency.

Particular attention is given to the gap between **ideal, feasible, and family-approved futures**.

## Research Ethics

Participation is voluntary and based on informed consent.

Identifying information, interview recordings, and participant-level responses are not publicly shared. Research materials are de-identified to protect privacy and confidentiality.

The study also avoids treating one participant's experience as representative of all female students in Kashgar or Xinjiang.

## Limitations

This project is designed as an exploratory case study rather than a representative survey.

The small sample does not support statistical generalization, retrospective accounts may be affected by memory, and the short fieldwork period prevents long-term observation of changing career plans.

The project's goal is therefore to identify possible social mechanisms and generate questions for future larger-scale research rather than estimate population-level patterns.
